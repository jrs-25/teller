# Bringing the Business Team Along

_Part 3 of the case study. The design is in [RFC.md](RFC.md)._

I'd be embedded with the Support lead and two veteran CSRs, one of whom thinks AI has no business in member conversations. My plan is three conversations, in order, and a very light ask of their time.

**First, agree on the problem.** Most of the day goes to tickets that don't need experienced judgment: the same CD question, the same password reset, forwarding feedback to Product. That volume is what makes the rare cases get slow, inconsistent handling. By the time a ticket with real nuance shows up, the CSR has already read two hundred routine ones. Teller exists to take the routine volume so the team's attention goes where it matters. In the same conversation we agree on what a good resolution looks like, in their words: the reply a veteran would send, which means accurate, citing a current help-center article, in Bankrate's voice, and never guessing about the member's account; and for routing, the right team with what that team needs to act. That definition is the bar every draft is reviewed against.

**Second, show them the rollout adds no extra work.** Teller runs behind the scenes first. Nobody sees it, and nothing in the queue changes. When it's right often enough, it starts leaving an internal note on the ticket: a draft or a routing suggestion with a one-line reason. The CSR can use it or ignore it. Nothing happens on their behalf until a category has earned it, with the Support lead deciding each step. Objecting is one click, and enough objections pull a category back automatically.

**Third, be clear about what gets measured.** We measure Teller: how often its draft was sent unchanged, how often a routed ticket was moved back, how much time it gave back. We don't measure how fast a CSR approves. A speed target would turn review into rubber-stamping, which is the one thing that makes a human gate worthless, and I'd say that to the team on day one.

**Where I expect friction.** The fear that this is a step toward replacing them or measuring them, which the third conversation answers directly. Drafts that sound generic or off-tone, which the recurring review catches and fixes at the source. And Compliance getting more gray-zone tickets than it wants while the compliance line is being set. The sharpest friction will come from the skeptic.

**The skeptic.** The goal is that they feel fully heard and see their input in the rollout, and that nothing is forced on them before Teller has earned it. That doesn't mean designing around them. They review Teller's disagreements with the team alongside the other CSRs in a brief, recurring review, and their objections get the same treatment as everyone's: those become rules, thresholds, and test cases they can point to. They may never like it. They need to trust that pushing back changes the system, and it's built so it does.

**Handoff.** The Support lead owns the config for general Q&A and product feedback, the promotion decisions, and the kill switch. The recurring review becomes theirs. Engineering owns the code. It's done when the review runs the same way the week after I leave.
